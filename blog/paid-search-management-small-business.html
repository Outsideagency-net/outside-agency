Paid Search

The Complete Guide to Paid Search Management for Small Businesses

By Outside Agency · November 9, 2026 · 5 min read

Paid search management is the ongoing work of running ads on Google and Bing so they bring in customers at a price you can afford. It covers which searches you show up for, what your ads say, how much you bid, and where the click lands. For a small business, the job is simple to describe and easy to get wrong.

This guide covers how the Google Ads auction actually works, what clicks and leads cost right now, where Local Services Ads fit, and how to tell if your account (or your agency) is doing a good job.

What paid search management actually involves

Setting up a campaign takes an afternoon. Managing one is the part that decides whether you make money. Most of the work happens after launch:

  • Reading the search terms people typed before they clicked, and blocking the ones that will never buy.
  • Testing ad copy against real results, not opinions.
  • Moving budget toward the services, hours, and areas that produce booked jobs.
  • Making sure every call and form fill is tracked, so decisions are based on leads instead of clicks.

If nobody is doing those four things every week or two, you have an ad account, not a managed one.

How Google decides whose ad shows first

A lot of business owners assume the biggest bid wins. It doesn't. Google runs an auction every time someone searches, and it ranks ads using a score called Ad Rank. According to Google's own explanation of the auction, Ad Rank looks at six things: your bid, the quality of your ad and landing page, the expected impact of your ad assets, minimum quality thresholds, the context of the search, and how competitive the auction is.

The part that matters most for a small budget is this line from the same page: "even if your competition has higher bids, you can still win a higher position at a lower price by using highly relevant keywords, ads, and assets."

In plain terms, a tight account with relevant ads and a page that matches the search can beat a bigger competitor who is lazy about it. That is where good management earns its fee.

What clicks and leads cost

LocaliQ publishes benchmarks from its customers' campaigns. Its 2026 search advertising benchmarks put the all-industry average at $5.42 per click, an 8.18% conversion rate on Google Ads, and $66.69 per lead.

Home services run higher. LocaliQ's home services report, based on 3,211 US campaigns from April 2024 to March 2025, found a median cost per click of $7.85 and a median cost per lead of $90.92.

Benchmarks are useful for spotting a broken account. They are not a goal. The number that matters is your break-even cost per lead, and you can work it out in a few minutes.

A quick break-even example

Say an average job is worth $1,200 in gross profit and you close one out of every four leads. Each lead is worth about $300 to you ($1,200 divided by 4). At $90 per lead you are doing well. At $350 you are losing money on every lead, no matter what the industry average says. Plug in your own numbers. They will tell you more than any benchmark.

Where Local Services Ads fit

If you are a plumber, HVAC company, electrician, roofer, or another home service business, you may also see Local Services Ads at the very top of Google. These work differently from regular search ads. As Google's Local Services help page puts it, "With Local Services Ads, you pay for valid leads," not clicks.

One detail catches people off guard. Under Google's rules, a missed call during business hours counts as a valid lead if the caller stays on the line for more than 20 seconds. If your phones go unanswered, you can pay for leads you never talked to.

These ads are also changing. Google says Local Services Ads are moving into Google Ads as a Performance Max campaign type that keeps the pay-per-lead model. The rollout started in August 2026 for select US home service advertisers, expands to service-area businesses and other groups in late 2026, and reaches non-US accounts and the remaining categories in 2027. Weekly budgets become daily budgets, and manual bidding is being retired. If you run these ads, check your settings after your account moves.

For many local service businesses, the best setup is both: Local Services Ads for pay-per-lead calls, and search ads for the specific services and areas you want more of.

The weekly work that saves the most money

If you only do one thing, read your search terms report. Google describes it as a list of the actual searches that triggered your ads, and its help page on negative keywords walks through adding the bad ones as exclusions straight from the report.

A roofing company bidding on "roof repair" will find searches like "roof repair jobs," "DIY roof repair," and "roof repair cost calculator" in there. None of those people are hiring a roofer today. Each click is money spent on someone who was never going to call.

After that, the next most valuable habits are:

  1. Track calls and forms as conversions. Without it, Google's automated bidding has no way to learn which clicks turn into customers.
  2. Send each service to its own page. Someone searching "water heater replacement" should land on a page about water heaters, not your homepage.
  3. Run ads when you can answer the phone. Or make sure someone (or something) picks up when you can't.
  4. Split campaigns by service. Emergency repair and new installs have different values, so they need different budgets.

How to judge your account or your agency

Here is a blunt test. If your monthly report leads with impressions and clicks, ask for three other numbers: cost per lead, number of booked jobs, and revenue from those jobs. An agency that can't connect ad spend to booked work is guessing, and you are paying for the guess.

Good questions to ask any paid search manager:

  • Who owns the Google Ads account if we part ways? (It should be you.)
  • How often do you review search terms and add negatives?
  • Which conversions are you tracking, and how are phone calls counted?
  • What is our cost per lead by service, and how has it changed?
  • What did you change last month, and what happened because of it?

What to expect in the first few months

Nobody can promise you a cost per lead before seeing your market, and you should be wary of anyone who does. The first month is mostly about collecting clean data: which searches convert, which services make money, and what your real lead cost is. After that, the work is steady trimming and testing.

The businesses that do best with paid search treat it like any other part of operations. They know their numbers, they answer the phone, and they expect a monthly report they can actually use.
